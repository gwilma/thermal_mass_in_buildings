How deep the daily wave reaches

By the end you can work out, for any material and any period, how deep a temperature swing gets into it. That one number tells you how thick a layer is worth having, and why a heatwave uses mass that an ordinary day doesn't.

A wave that dies as it goes in

In lesson 1 we assumed the top 100 mm of the soffit warmed evenly by 2 K and the rest stayed put. Reality is smoother. The surface swings up and down once a day. That swing travels into the slab as a wave, and the wave shrinks and falls behind as it goes deeper.

The reason is how diffusion scales. Heat spreading through a solid covers a distance that grows with the square root of time, not with time itself. In a day, heat gets only so far before the surface has turned round and started pulling it back outThis square-root scaling falls out of the heat diffusion equation. You'll derive it in lesson 14. Carslaw & Jaeger, Conduction of Heat in Solids (1959), chapter 2, gives the periodic solution used here..

For a thick solid whose surface swings by ±θ̂ with period T, the temperature at depth x is:

θ(x,t) = θ̂ e−x/δ cos(ωt − x/δ)
K
ω = 2π/T. The e−x/δ part shrinks the swing; the −x/δ inside the cosine delays it.

Everything hangs on one length, the penetration depth:

δ = √(λT)√(πρc) = √(αT/π),    α = λ/ρc
m
T in seconds: 86,400 for a day. α is the thermal diffusivity, m²/s: how fast a temperature change spreads.
The idea to keep

At one penetration depth the swing is down to 37% and arrives 3.8 hours late on a daily cycle. At three, it is down to 5%. Below about 3δ, the material might as well not be there for that cycle.

Try it. Change the material and the period, and drag the time slider to watch the wave move in. The dashed lines are the envelope e−x/δ; the red line is the temperature right now.

Things to notice. Dense concrete on a daily cycle: δ ≈ 150 mm. CLT: about 70 mm. Switch to 7 days and concrete's δ grows to about 400 mm, because δ grows with √T. The trap: mineral wool

Now pick mineral wool. Its penetration depth on a daily cycle is about 175 mm, deeper than concrete's. Yet nobody counts insulation as thermal mass. What's going on?

Diffusivity says how far a change spreads, not how much heat moves. Mineral wool has almost no heat capacity to fill, so the wave runs in fast and carries almost nothing. The property that measures how much heat a surface takes in for a given swing is the thermal effusivityEffusivity is why a concrete floor feels cold underfoot and a carpet doesn't, at the same temperature. The concrete draws heat from your foot faster.:

b = √(λρc),    q̂s = b√ω θ̂s
J/m²K s½
q̂s is the amplitude of heat flux into the surface for a surface swing θ̂s. Concrete: b ≈ 2,190. Mineral wool: about 33.

So you need two properties, not one. Diffusivity sets how deep the wave goes. Effusivity sets how much heat it carries. Good thermal mass is high in both: dense concrete, block, brick, rammed earth.

What this means for thickness

For a layer backed by insulation, the useful storage on a daily cycle rises with thickness up to about 0.7 to 1 δ, then levels off and even dips slightly as the wave reflects off the insulation. For dense concrete the peak is at about 100 to 110 mm, and 90% of the peak comes from the first 55 mm or so. For CLT the peak is near 75 mmCalculated with the project's EN ISO 13786 calculator (physics/iso13786.py, which matches the standard's Annex D test case exactly) for a layer on 100 mm PIR with Rsi = 0.13. Lesson 4 shows how.. That is where the "first 100 mm" rule comes from, and it is a daily-cycle rule. Over a heatwave, the deeper mass joins in.

Practice

Use the calculator in the sidebar (the Calculator button on a phone). T = 86,400 s for a day.

Exercise 1 · penetration depth

Brick: λ = 0.77 W/mK, ρ = 1,700 kg/m³, c = 800 J/kgK. What is its penetration depth on a daily cycle, in mm?

δ = √(0.77 × 86,400 ÷ (π × 1,700 × 800)) = √0.01557 = 0.125 m = 125 mm. In the calculator: sqrt(0.77*86400/(pi*1700*800))*1000.

Exercise 2 · scaling with period

Dense concrete has δ = 151 mm on a 24-hour cycle. Without the full formula, what is it on a 7-day cycle?

δ grows with √T. Seven times the period gives √7 = 2.65 times the depth: 151 × 2.65 = 400 mm.

Exercise 3 · the swing inside

The soffit surface swings ±2 K over the day. How big is the swing 100 mm into the concrete (δ = 151 mm)?

2 × e−100/151 = 2 × 0.516 = ±1.03 K, about half the surface swing. It also arrives (100/151) × 24/(2π) = 2.5 hours late. So in lesson 1, "the top 100 mm warms by 2 K" overstated the storage. Lesson 4 gives the exact figure.

Exercise 4 · effusivity

For thick dense concrete (b = 2,190) on a daily cycle, how much heat flux goes into the surface per kelvin of surface swing? That is, b√ω.

ω = 2π/86,400 = 7.27×10−5 rad/s, so √ω = 0.00853 and b√ω = 18.7 W/m²K. Compare 1/Rsi = 7.7 W/m²K from lesson 1. The concrete could take heat more than twice as fast as the air film can deliver it. That mismatch is lesson 3.

Check your recall

How does penetration depth change with the period of the swing?

  1. In proportion to the period
  2. With the period squared
  3. With the square root of period
  4. It does not depend on period
δ = √(αT/π). Four times the period, twice the depth.

Mineral wool has a larger penetration depth than concrete. Why is it still useless as thermal mass?

  1. Its effusivity is tiny
  2. Its diffusivity is tiny
  3. Its conductivity is huge
  4. Its density is huge
With almost no heat capacity, b = √(λρc) is about 33 against concrete's 2,190. The wave goes deep but carries almost no heat.

On a daily cycle, roughly how much of the surface swing is left at three penetration depths?

  1. About 37 percent
  2. About 50 percent
  3. About 14 percent
  4. About 5 percent
e−3 = 0.05. At one depth it is e−1 = 37%.

Why does a week-long heatwave use deeper mass than a single hot day?

  1. The surface gets much hotter
  2. The longer period reaches deeper
  3. The concrete's diffusivity rises
  4. The air film gets much thinner
Penetration depth grows with √T: about 150 mm for a day and 400 mm for a week in dense concrete.
