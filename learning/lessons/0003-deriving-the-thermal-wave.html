Lesson 3 · Derivation

Deriving the thermal wave

Lesson 2 handed you the wave formula, the penetration depth and the effusivity. Here you derive all three from two physical facts: energy is conserved, and heat flows down a temperature gradient. Nothing on this page is taken on trust.

You need partial derivatives and the complex exponential eiφ = cos φ + i sin φ. Each step has a prompt: try the step yourself on paper before opening it. Working it out first is what makes it stick.

Step 1 · Energy balance on a thin slice

Take a slab, and look at a thin slice of it between depth x and x + dx, with face area A. Heat flux q(x) flows in through one face and q(x+dx) flows out through the other. Whatever doesn't come out stays in the slice and warms it.

Try it: write the energy balance for the slice, then let dx shrink to zero.
ρc A dx ∂θ∂t = A [q(x) − q(x+dx)]   ⇒   ρc ∂θ∂t = −∂q∂x
(1)
Divide by A dx; as dx → 0, [q(x+dx) − q(x)]/dx becomes ∂q/∂x. In words: a slice warms when more heat enters than leaves.

Step 2 · Add Fourier's law

Lesson 1's conduction law, written for a point rather than a whole layer, says heat flows down the gradient: q = −λ ∂θ/∂x.

Try it: substitute this into equation (1), assuming λ doesn't vary with depth.
ρc ∂θ∂t = λ ∂²θ∂x²   ⇒   ∂θ∂t = α ∂²θ∂x²,   α = λρc
(2)
This is the heat diffusion equation. Temperature rises where the profile curves upwards (∂²θ/∂x² > 0), because more heat flows in from the warmer side than leaves on the cooler side. Diffusivity α appears naturally as the one material property that sets the pace.

Step 3 · Why the square root of time

Before solving anything, look at the units. α is in m²/s. If the only things in the problem are α and a time t, the only length you can build from them is √(αt). So whatever the exact answer, the distance heat reaches must scale as √(αt). Four times the time, twice the depthThis kind of argument is dimensional analysis. It gives the scaling but not the constant in front, which is why the full solution is still needed..

Step 4 · Set up the periodic problem

Now the building case. The surface (x = 0) swings as θ(0,t) = θ cos ωt, with ω = 2π/T, and it has been doing so long enough that start-up effects have died away. The slab is thick enough to treat as going on for ever.

The trick is to write the cosine as the real part of a complex exponential, cos ωt = Re(eiωt), and look for a solution where every depth oscillates at the same frequency, just with its own size and timing:

θ(x,t) = Re[ Θ(x) eiωt ]
(3)
Θ(x) is a complex amplitude. Its size is the swing at depth x; its angle is the timing.

This works because equation (2) is linear: real and imaginary parts don't mix, so we can solve with the complex form and take the real part at the end.

Try it: substitute (3) into (2). What ordinary differential equation does Θ(x) satisfy?

The time derivative brings down iω; the space derivative acts only on Θ:

iω Θ = α Θ″   ⇒   Θ″ = k²Θ,   k² = iωα
(4)

Step 5 · Solve it

Equation (4) has solutions Θ = Ae−kx + Be+kx. The only new piece is the square root of i.

Try it: what is √i? (Hint: write i = eiπ/2.)

√i = eiπ/4 = (1 + i)/√2. So

k = (1 + i) √(ω/2α) = 1 + iδ,    δ = √(2α/ω) = √(αT/π)
(5)
That is the penetration depth from lesson 2, appearing on its own. Substituting α = λ/ρc gives δ = √(λT/πρc).
Try it: use the two boundary conditions to find A and B.

Deep in the slab the swing can't grow without limit, but e+kx grows as ex/δ. So B = 0. At the surface Θ(0) = θ, so A = θ.

Try it: put it back together and take the real part.
θ = Re[ θ e−x/δ e−ix/δ eiωt ] = θ e−x/δ cos(ωt − x/δ)
(6)
The real part of k gives the decay, e−x/δ. The imaginary part gives the delay, a phase of x/δ radians, which is (x/δ) · T/2π in time. Both come from the same (1 + i), which is why decay and delay always go together.
The idea to keep

The thermal wave is just the heat diffusion equation driven at one frequency. Decay and delay both come from √i = (1 + i)/√2: equal parts real and imaginary, so the swing loses a factor e and a radian of phase over every penetration depth.

Step 6 · The heat flux at the surface

Thermal mass is about heat, not temperature, so finish with the flux into the surface. Use Fourier's law at x = 0 on the complex form.

Try it: find q(0,t) = −λ ∂θ/∂x at the surface. What is its size, and how is it timed against the surface temperature?

Differentiating θe−kx brings down −k, so the complex flux amplitude is λkθ = λ(1 + i)θ/δ.

|qs| = √2 λδ θ = λ√(ω/α) θ = √(λρc) √ω θ = b√ω θ
(7)
Effusivity b = √(λρc) falls out of the algebra; nobody had to invent it. The factor (1 + i) has an angle of 45°, so the flux leads the surface temperature by one eighth of a cycle: 3 hours on a daily cycle. Heat goes in fastest while the surface is still warming, not at its peak.

What the derivation assumed

Every assumption is a limit you can now name:

  • One dimension. Heat flows straight in. Fine for walls and slabs away from edges and corners.
  • Constant properties. λ, ρ and c don't change with temperature or moisture. Phase change materials break this.
  • A single, steady sinusoid. Real weather is not a cosine. Because the equation is linear, any repeating pattern can be split into a sum of sinusoids (24 h, 12 h, 8 h…) and each solved this way. That is what the admittance method does.
  • A semi-infinite solid. Real layers end, often against insulation. Then B ≠ 0: a reflected wave comes back, which is why storage peaks at about 0.7 to 1 δ and dips slightly beyond. Keeping both terms for each layer gives the ISO 13786 transfer matrix, which you'll derive in lesson 16.
  • Surface temperature imposed. In a room, the surface temperature isn't given; it depends on the air film. Adding that film is lesson 4.

Exercise 1 · penetration depth from α and ω

Dense concrete has α = 0.833 mm²/s. Using δ = √(2α/ω) with a 24-hour period, what is δ in mm?

ω = 2π/86,400 = 7.27×10−5 rad/s. δ = √(2 × 0.833×10−6 ÷ 7.27×10−5) = √0.0229 = 0.151 m = 151 mm. Calculator: sqrt(2*0.833e-6/(2pi/86400))*1000.

Exercise 2 · the flux lead

From equation (7), the surface flux leads the surface temperature by 45°. How many hours is that on a daily cycle?

45° is 1/8 of 360°, so 24/8 = 3 hours. The project's physics note quotes the same figure (section 3.2).

Exercise 3 · flux for brick

Brick: λ = 0.77, ρ = 1,700, c = 800. Using b√ω, how much heat flux per kelvin of surface swing does thick brick take on a daily cycle?

b = √(0.77 × 1,700 × 800) = 1,023. √ω = 0.00853. b√ω = 8.72 W/m²K, under half of concrete's 18.7.

Exercise 4 · check the formula two ways

Equation (7) says b√ω also equals √2 λ/δ. Check it for concrete: λ = 2.0 W/mK, δ = 0.151 m.

√2 × 2.0 ÷ 0.151 = 18.7 W/m²K, the same as b√ω from lesson 2. One reading: the surface flux is what you'd get by conducting across a layer δ/√2 thick.

Check your recall

Which two facts does the heat diffusion equation combine?

  1. Fourier's law and Newton's cooling law
  2. Energy conservation and Fourier's law
  3. Energy conservation and Newton's cooling law
  4. Fourier's law and the Stefan–Boltzmann law
A slice warms when more heat enters than leaves (conservation), and heat flows down the gradient (Fourier).

Why is the e+kx solution thrown away for a thick slab?

  1. It breaks the surface condition
  2. It has the wrong frequency
  3. It has no imaginary part
  4. It grows without limit inward
Its size grows as ex/δ, which is impossible deep in a slab. In a finite layer it is kept, as the reflected wave.

Where do the decay and the delay of the thermal wave both come from?

  1. The square root of i
  2. The surface air film
  3. The slab's finite depth
  4. The changing conductivity
k = (1 + i)/δ. The real part decays the swing and the imaginary part delays it, by equal amounts.

On a daily cycle, when does heat flow into a thick concrete surface fastest?

  1. At the surface temperature peak
  2. Three hours after the peak
  3. Three hours before the peak
  4. Six hours after the peak
