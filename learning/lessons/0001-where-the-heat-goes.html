Lesson 1 · Fundamentals

Where the heat goes: four flows and one store

By the end you can write the heat balance for a room and put a number in watts on each term. That is the frame every later lesson hangs on, and the frame you'll use to judge any claim about thermal mass at ReMass.

Before you read: guess

A 30 m² office has an exposed concrete soffit. Both the room air and the top 100 mm of the soffit warm by 2 K over an afternoon. How many times more heat does the concrete take up than the air? Guess quickly; being wrong first helps you remember the answer.

One room, five things happening

Picture that office on a sunny afternoon. Heat arrives from the sun and from people and computers. It leaves through the walls and with the air. Some of it goes into the fabric and comes back later. Every one of those is one of four transfer mechanisms, plus storage.

The four flows

Conduction: heat through solids

Heat moves through a solid from hot to cold at a rate set by its thermal conductivity, λ. For a flat layer of thickness d this is Fourier's lawFourier's law and the other three rate laws on this page are set out in chapter 1 of Lienhard & Lienhard, A Heat Transfer Textbook (6th ed., 2024, free from MIT).:

q = λ Δθd = ΔθR,    R = d/λ
W/m²
q heat flux, W/m²; λ conductivity, W/(m·K); d thickness, m; R thermal resistance, m²K/W; Δθ temperature difference, K.

Resistances in series add, so a whole wall becomes one number, the U-value: U = 1/(Rsi + Σd/λ + Rse), and the steady loss is U A Δθ. A modern wall at U = 0.2 W/m²K with 10 m² exposed and a 10 K difference loses just 20 W. Keep that small number in mind.

Convection: heat between a surface and the air

Air moving over a surface picks up or gives up heat. In building calculations it is written as Newton's law of cooling, with a coefficient hc that wraps up all the fluid mechanics:

q = hc (θair − θsurface)
W/m²
Indoors, with still air, hc is small: roughly 1.5 to 3 W/m²K on a wall, more when air is blown across the surface.

That small coefficient is the first hint of a bottleneck. Measured values scatter widely with air speed, surface orientation and temperature differencePeeters, Beausoleil-Morrison & Novoselac (2011), Internal convective heat transfer modeling: critical review, Energy and Buildings 43. Different simulation tools pick different correlations, one source of the model spread you'll meet in lesson 6..

Radiation: heat between surfaces, through the air

Every surface emits longwave infrared. A warm sunlit floor radiates to a cooler ceiling without warming the air between them. Near room temperature the exchange is close to linear, so it is written the same way as convectionThe linear coefficient comes from differentiating the Stefan–Boltzmann law: hr ≈ 4εσT³. With ε = 0.9 and T = 293 K this is 5.1 W/m²K. Lienhard & Lienhard ch. 1 and ch. 10.:

q ≈ hr (θ1 − θ2),    hr ≈ 5 W/m²K
W/m²

Sunlight is radiation too, but shortwave: it passes through glass and lands as a heat patch on whatever it hits, usually the floor. That is the most direct way into a slab, with no air in the way.

Hand calculations lump convection and longwave radiation at a room surface into one surface resistance. The standard value for a wall is Rsi = 0.13 m²K/W, which is a combined coefficient of 1/0.13 ≈ 7.7 W/m²KEN ISO 6946:2017 gives Rsi = 0.10 (heat flowing up), 0.13 (horizontal) and 0.17 m²K/W (heat flowing down). The project's physics note, section 2.2, has the breakdown..

Ventilation: heat carried by air

Air leaving the room takes its heat with it; air entering brings outdoor temperature in. Air holds about 1.2 kJ per cubic metre per kelvin, which is 0.33 Wh/m³K, hence the familiar ruleThe 0.33 is 1.2 kg/m³ × 1,005 J/kgK ÷ 3,600 s/h, rounded down. The Open University's free course Energy in buildings, section 3.3 derives it.:

Φv ≈ 0.33 n V (θin − θout)
W
n air changes per hour; V room volume, m³.
Quick recall: cover the page above. Which of the four flows needs no air at all?

Radiation, both the sun's shortwave and the longwave exchange between surfaces. Conduction needs no air either, but it runs through solids, not across the room. Convection and ventilation both work through the air.

The store

Anything with mass holds heat. Warming a material by Δθ stores

Q = ρ c d A Δθ = C Δθ
J
ρ density, kg/m³; c specific heat capacity, J/(kg·K); C heat capacity, J/K. Dense concrete is about ρ = 2,400 and c = 1,000, so ρc = 2.4 MJ/m³K.

Now compare the two stores in the office. The air: 81 m³ × 1.2 kJ/m³K = 98 kJ/K. The top 100 mm of soffit: 30 m² × 0.1 m × 2.4 MJ/m³K = 7,200 kJ/K. The concrete holds about 74 times as much per kelvin. You guessed (no guess locked in).

Why only the top 100 mm? Because on a daily cycle the deeper concrete barely changes temperature. Lesson 2 shows why and how to work out that depth for any material. For now, take it as given.

The balance

Put the flows and the store together. Treat the room as one temperature θi, the outside as θe, and group the losses into one heat loss coefficient H (W/K):

Φgains = H (θi − θe) + C dθidt,    H = ΣUA + 0.33nV
W
Gains in = heat leaking out + heat going into storage.
The idea to keep

The storage term is C times the rate of change of temperature. If the temperature doesn't move, the mass stores nothing, however much of it there is.

That gives the three conditions this course is built on. Thermal mass only does useful work when there is capacity (this lesson), access for heat to get in and out in time (lessons 2 to 4), and a temperature swing to drive it (lessons 5 and 7)The same three conditions open the project's physics note, section 1..

Exercise 1 · storage

Over the afternoon the top 100 mm of the 30 m² soffit warms by 2 K. How much heat has it stored, in kWh?

Q = 7,200 kJ/K × 2 K = 14,400 kJ = 14.4 MJ. Since 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ, Q = 4.0 kWh. The room air over the same 2 K takes 0.2 MJ, or 0.05 kWh.

Exercise 2 · surface exchange

The room sits 3 K warmer than the soffit surface. Using Rsi = 0.13 m²K/W, how fast does heat flow into the 30 m² soffit, in watts?

q = 3 K ÷ 0.13 = 23 W/m². Over 30 m² that is about 690 W.

Exercise 3 · put them together

At that rate, how many hours does it take to put the 4.0 kWh from exercise 1 into the soffit?

4.0 kWh ÷ 0.69 kW = 5.8 hours, most of a working day. The concrete could hold heat faster than that; the thin film of air at its surface is what sets the pace. Lesson 3 is about this throttle.

Exercise 4 · ventilation

At night the windows are opened to 4 air changes per hour, and outside air is 6 K cooler than the room. How much heat does the air carry away, in watts?

0.33 × 4 × 81 m³ × 6 K = about 640 W. That is the same order as the 690 W the soffit can give up through its surface. Night cooling is a chain of two links, and the weaker one sets the rate. Compare the 20 W through the wall: in a well-insulated building, ventilation and surface exchange are the flows that matter for mass.

In the office, which heat store hardly matters over an afternoon?

  1. The room air
  2. The floor slab
  3. The soffit slab
  4. The block walls
The air holds about 98 kJ/K against 7,200 kJ/K in the top 100 mm of soffit. Air sets how fast heat moves, not how much is stored.

An office is held at exactly 21 °C day and night by its heating and cooling. Roughly how much heat does its slab take in and give back each day?

  1. About four kWh
  2. About forty kWh
  3. About zero kWh
  4. About one kWh
The storage term is C dθ/dt. With no temperature change there is nothing to drive heat in or out, so the mass does almost nothing. This is the third condition: mass needs a swing.

A surface resistance of 0.13 m²K/W is the same as a combined surface coefficient of about:

  1. 1.3 W/m²K
  2. 7.7 W/m²K
  3. 5.0 W/m²K
  4. 3.3 W/m²K
1 ÷ 0.13 = 7.7 W/m²K: roughly 5 from longwave radiation and 2.5 from convection.

What does 0.33 nV Δθ calculate?

  1. Heat through the wall
  2. Heat into the slab
  3. Heat from the people
  4. Heat carried by air
It is the ventilation heat flow. 0.33 Wh/m³K is the heat capacity of air.

In exercise 3, what limited how fast the soffit could charge?

  1. The surface film resistance
  2. The concrete's specific heat
  3. The air's heat capacity
  4. The slab's overall thickness
The surface resistance of 0.13 m²K/W allowed only 23 W/m² for a 3 K difference. Doubling the concrete would not change that rate.
