Thermal mass and building physics

Twenty short lessons in three parts: the physics, using it in practice, and building the models yourself. Results are derived before they are used. Each lesson ends with practice that checks itself, and each feeds a one-page reference sheet.

The aim is a full understanding of thermal mass, from first principles to real projects and your own code. Part A comes first so the physics is in place before ReMass on 29 October. The order is a draft and will shift as we find out what you already know.

Part A · The physics

  1. 01Where the heat goes: four flows and one storereadyConduction, convection, radiation, ventilation and storage. Writing the room heat balance and sizing each term in watts.
  2. 02How deep the daily wave reachesreadyDiffusivity, effusivity and penetration depth. Why only the first 100 mm or so of concrete works on a daily cycle.
  3. 03Deriving the thermal wavereadyFrom energy conservation and Fourier's law to the heat diffusion equation, the periodic solution, penetration depth and effusivity.
  4. 04The surface throttlenextDerive admittance with an air film in the way, and the 7.7 W/m²K ceiling. Why exposure and air movement beat thickness.
  5. 05Reading a build-up: ISO 13786 numbersplannedAdmittance, κ1, decrement factor and time lag. What linings, carpets and dabs do.
  6. 06The whole building as a circuitplannedDerive the time constant τ = C/H and the attenuation of swings. Preheat and demand flexibility.
  7. 07How models represent massplannedAdmittance method, 5R1C, the Home Energy Model's five nodes, finite difference. Where they disagree, and the Roberts et al. test houses.
  8. 08When mass doesn't helpplannedConstant setpoints, warm nights, bedrooms, intermittent heating, and the physics of diminishing returns.

Part B · Practice

  1. 09Estimating a summer peak by handplannedThe CIBSE cyclic (admittance) method: mean plus swing, for a room you could be asked about tomorrow.
  2. 10Designing the night purgeplannedAirflow, surface coupling and night temperatures. Sizing openings so the mass actually discharges.
  3. 11Heating, intermittency and preheatplannedWhen mass saves heating energy, when it costs it, and gain utilisation.
  4. 12Mass as a battery: demand flexibilityplannedHow long a heat pump can switch off, and what sets the answer.
  5. 13Reading overheating resultsplannedTM52, TM59, Approved Document O and weather files. Which inputs move the answer most.
  6. 14Judging claims about massplannedCase studies, the diminishing-returns literature and embodied carbon. Spotting a benefit that won't happen.

Part C · Building the models

  1. 15A finite-difference wall in codeplannedWrite one in Python, derive its stability limit, and check it against the analytical solution from lesson 3.
  2. 16The ISO 13786 transfer matrixplannedDerive it from the finite-layer solution, code it, and match ISO 13786 Annex D exactly.
  3. 17RC networks from 1R1C to 5R1CplannedBuild a zone model, run it on real weather, and see what each node buys you.
  4. 18Lumping errors: ISO 52016 and HEMplannedFive-node elements against the exact solution, and why linings and timber come out wrong.
  5. 19Inside whole-building simulationplannedConduction transfer functions, convection algorithms, solar distribution and furniture.
  6. 20Validation and calibrationplannedBESTEST, test houses and grey-box identification: how to know a model is right.

Main sources

  • EN ISO 13786:2017 and CIBSE Guide A (2015), chapter 5: the standard methods for dynamic thermal properties and the admittance method.
  • HEM-TP-07 v2.0: how the Home Energy Model treats thermal mass.
  • Roberts et al. (2019), Predictions of summertime overheating, BSERT 40(4): models against measured test houses.
  • The project's own physics note, ISO 13786 calculator and models, which have been checked against ISO 13786 Annex D and ASHRAE 140.
